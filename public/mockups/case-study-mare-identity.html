Visual identity, MARE

An identity system built around archive, not app. Four colours, two typefaces, and a 64-pin field-marker set drawn from the I-Ching — designed to register as a working tool from the field of critical theory rather than a software product.

01 — The brief

A platform that reads as field notes, not feed.

MARE began as a research platform for navigating fragmented media archives by semantic proximity rather than recommendation. The identity had to do the same work as the product: refuse the visual language of optimisation — soft gradients, friendly mascots, app-store sheen — and instead borrow from the surfaces archives actually have. Card catalogues. Field diaries. Library stamps.

Two parallel audiences: critical theorists who would never trust a "platform" that looked like one, and engineers who would dismiss anything overly precious. The system had to read as serious and built from both directions.

Constraint No mascots, no rounded corners, no gradient sheens, no stock illustration. The visual system reads as 'instrument' or it doesn't ship.
Pivot First round leaned soft and warm. Tested with five readers from each audience — all called it 'a Notion clone.' Reset.
Lock Carbon black + alabaster as base; pink + green as instrument signals (link, state). One serif, one utility sans. No more.
02 — Wordmark

Set in Libre Baskerville, with the dot of the ‘i’ falling deliberately low.

The wordmark is a single line of Libre Baskerville at its untouched proportions — no kerning gymnastics, no glyph remix. The intent: the wordmark should feel set, not designed. Like a chapter header.

03 — Palette

Four colours, each with a job.

No tints, no shades, no expanded gradient ramp. Four hex values that each do one thing, used consistently everywhere they appear.

Carbon#141810
Page, frame, ground
Alabaster#E6E6EA
Body text, primary surface
Hot pink#FF6392
Wordmark, link, division
Acid green#98CE00
State, instrument, label
04 — Pin / field-marker system

64 markers, drawn from the I-Ching.

Every item in the archive needs a pin: a small, recognisable mark that holds position on the field. Rather than invent 64 abstract glyphs, the system borrows the hexagrams — six lines, broken or solid — yielding 64 unique markers with a built-in numeric order. The first eight shown here.

06 — Reflection

The hardest constraint turned out to be productive: by refusing the visual vocabulary of software, the system ended up reading as infrastructure — which is what MARE wanted to be anyway.
